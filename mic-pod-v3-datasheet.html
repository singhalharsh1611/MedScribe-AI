A symmetric tabletop capsule with a microphone at each rounded end, facing opposite directions. It sits between two seating groups — patient and family on one side, clinician on the other — and each end captures its own side. Voice-gated local recording to microSD, dumped on demand over BLE to an iOS app. A single button at the centre of the top face mutes and unmutes both microphones.

02 Enclosure

The body is a stadium-section capsule: a single master solid mirrored about the vertical centre plane, then split horizontally into a top and bottom shell. Both ends are geometrically identical, so the device has no front and no back — that symmetry is what lets it sit between two parties without appearing to belong to either.

Mute needs a state indicator

A mute button with no feedback is a button you cannot trust — you press it and have no way to know which state you are in. No new geometry is needed: the existing side LED can carry it, with steady for live and slow-blink or a second colour for muted. Whatever the scheme, mute state and recording state must be distinguishable at a glance from across the desk, because in this setting the indicator is doing consent work, not decoration.

03 Acoustic path

Each end carries a bored waveguide from the grille face down to the microphone, which sits on a PCB rotated 20° above horizontal. The tilt is the whole point: a seated talker's mouth is roughly 0.40 m above the tabletop, so a flat-facing port at 10 mm height would aim at everyone's chest.

The tube resonance is clear of speech

The first quarter-wave mode of a 12 mm bore lands at 6.5 kHz, comfortably above the 300–3400 Hz band that carries intelligibility and above the 4 kHz where most fricative energy sits. A longer bore would drag that peak down into the speech band; a shorter one would push it past the 8 kHz Nyquist limit and out of the recording entirely. 12 mm is in the right place, and it is worth not changing it casually.

The third limit nobody quotes

Beyond the critical distance the reverberant field dominates the direct sound. Moving further away stops reducing the level much, but smears the signal — and reverberation hurts transcription accuracy in a way raw SNR does not capture.

Critical distance, Dc = 0.057 √(Q·V / RT₆₀)
RoomVolumeRT₆₀Q = 1Q = 2
Consult room, 4 × 4 × 2.7 m, soft furnishing43.2 m³0.5 s0.53 m0.75 m
Same room, bare walls43.2 m³0.7 s0.45 m0.63 m
Small office30.0 m³0.4 s0.49 m0.70 m

Q = 2 is the right column here — a talker seated at a table radiates into a half-space rather than a full sphere. So Dc ≈ 0.75 m.

What this means in practice

The pod hears cleanly to about 0.75 m, works well to 1.8 m in a quiet room, and degrades gracefully past that. Across a normal 1.2 m consultation desk, both parties land inside the good band. In a noisy clinic at 45–50 dBA the usable radius collapses to 0.5–1.0 m, and people will need to be closer than feels natural.

A raised voice at 66 dBA buys back range — 3.55 m in a 40 dBA room — but you cannot design around asking distressed patients to project.

The I²S clock problem

The nRF52840's I²S master clock derives from a fixed 32 MHz divider chain, and 16.000 kHz is not on it. With the RATIO = 64 that the INMP441's 32-bit frame requires:

MCK dividerMCKResulting fsErrorDrift
32 MHz / 311.0323 MHz16 129.0 Hz+0.81 %29.0 s/h
32 MHz / 321.0000 MHz15 625.0 Hz−2.34 %84.4 s/h
32 MHz / 301.0667 MHz16 666.7 Hz+4.17 %150.0 s/h
Pick 16 129 Hz and write it down

The /31 divider is the closest available. Half a percent of pitch error is inaudible and harmless to ASR, but the 29 s/h timing drift is not harmless if you ever want to align a transcript to wall-clock time or correlate two recordings. Two fixes, both cheap: stamp each segment header with the true 16 129 Hz rate and let the consumer resample, or timestamp segment boundaries from the RTC and correct in post. What you must not do is label the file 16 000 Hz.

The microSD card is the design's power problem

1.6 mA of the 5.02 mA recording current is still the card. Power-gating it through a load switch and buffering writes into RAM — batch 32 kB instead of 4 kB, wake the card once every 4 s instead of twice a second — would cut recording current to roughly 4.2 mA. Moving from ADPCM to Opus already took the larger share of this saving; power-gating is what is left.

Opus at 16 kbps, and the 80-second problem disappears

Against ADPCM it is four times smaller on every axis: a consultation drops from 4.8 MB to 1.2 MB, transfer from 80 s to 20 s measured — under 7 s at the protocol ceiling — and card capacity rises from 552 h to 2 208 h, which is 92 days of recorded speech.

There is no quality argument against it for this application. 16 kbps wideband Opus is designed for exactly this content, iOS decodes it natively, and every mainstream ASR stack takes it directly. If you want margin, 24 kbps still clears a consultation in 30 s.

Compression also makes recording cheaper

The counter-intuitive result. Opus costs more CPU than ADPCM — roughly 40 % duty against 25 % — but it writes a quarter as much to the card, and in §06 the microSD was 54 % of the recording budget. The write saving is larger than the CPU cost:

On-card structure

  • Segments written as timestamped Opus files, one per VAD-gated utterance run.
  • Store the encoded stream, never PCM — the card holds what the phone will fetch, so a transfer is a file copy with no transcode step on either end.
  • A manifest indexing {id, timestamp, duration, offset, crc}, so the phone can list without walking the filesystem.
  • Segment headers must carry the true 16 129 Hz sample rate, per §05. Opus resamples internally to 16 kHz, so the 0.81 % error has to be recorded in the container or it is lost.

09 Offload link

BLE 2M PHY runs at a 2 Mbps symbol rate. That is the radio's bit rate over the air, not the rate audio moves — the link spends a fixed slice of every packet cycle on protocol overhead that does not get faster when the PHY does.

What the protocol actually allows

BLE is strictly alternating. Every data packet is followed by a 150 µs inter-frame space, an acknowledgement from the central, and a second 150 µs space before the next packet. Those 300 µs are fixed by the specification and do not scale with symbol rate, so they cost proportionally more on the fast PHY. With Data Length Extension at a 251-byte PDU, one notification cycle looks like this:

Packet cycle, 251-byte PDU, encrypted
PHYPacket on airIFS + ack + IFSCycleATT payload
1M PHY2120.0 µs380.0 µs2500.0 µs244 B
2M PHY1064.0 µs344.0 µs1408.0 µs244 B

The 251-byte PDU carries a 4-byte L2CAP header and a 3-byte ATT notification header, leaving 244 bytes of audio per cycle. Divide that by the cycle time and the ceiling falls out:

Throughput, best case to realistic
PHYSymbol rateProtocol ceilingEfficiencyiOS-limitedMeasured
1M PHY1 Mbps97.6 kB/s
0.78 Mbps		78 %65.1 kB/s≈25 kB/s
2M PHY2 Mbps173.3 kB/s
1.39 Mbps		69 %97.6 kB/s≈55–65 kB/s
2 Mbps buys 1.39 Mbps, at best

173 kB/s is the hard ceiling on 2M PHY — 69 % of the symbol rate, with the missing 31 % spent on preamble, addressing, CRC, the MIC, and the two mandatory inter-frame spaces. Notice the fast PHY is the less efficient one: halving the air time does nothing to the fixed 300 µs of silence per cycle, so that overhead becomes a larger share of the total.

Reaching the ceiling needs every one of: 2M PHY negotiated, DLE at the full 251-byte PDU, an ATT MTU of 247 or more, connection events packed back to back with no idle time, and no retransmissions. Miss any and you land in the iOS-limited column — which is what a phone granting a 15 ms interval and six notifications per event gives you.

Sync per consultation, not per day

A single consultation clears in 20 seconds at the measured rate, under 7 at the ceiling — fast enough to finish while the clinician is still closing their notes, with the pod sitting on the desk. Even a full accumulated day is 16 minutes, which is recoverable rather than abandoned. Opus turns offload from a constraint on how the device is used into a background detail.

So the app should pull segments opportunistically: connect whenever the phone is near, fetch what is new, delete what is confirmed. Design the transfer resumable and idempotent — per-segment FETCH with a CRC per chunk means an interrupted sync costs one segment, not the session. Treat the 8-hour row as the failure case the design exists to prevent, not a normal mode of operation.

Plan against the measured column, not the ceiling. The gap between them is not inefficiency you can engineer away — it is the central deciding the connection interval and how many packets it will accept per event, and on iOS the peripheral only requests, it does not choose. If the phone declines 2M PHY entirely, a one-hour backlog goes from 2.0 minutes to 4.8. That asymmetry is the strongest argument for syncing little and often rather than relying on throughput.

GATT service

  • Control (write) — LIST, FETCH:<id>, DELETE:<id>
  • Data (notify) — chunked transfer, sequence number and CRC per chunk
  • Status (notify) — battery %, storage remaining, recording state

iOS side is a standard CoreBluetooth central: scan, connect, discover, subscribe to Data and Status, LIST, then iterate FETCH and reassemble with CRC checking into the app sandbox.

10 Bill of materials & fabrication

There is no printer in-house, so both shells, the button cap and the two grille discs are ordered from a Hyderabad bureau. That turns the enclosure from a spool-of-filament line item into the second-largest cost after the MCU — but the bigger consequence is that it decides which processes are on the table, and one feature of this design rules most of them out.

The grille decides the process

Each grille disc carries 37 holes at ⌀0.55 mm. Measured against published minimum-feature guidance, that single number eliminates every powder and filament process:

Split the order across two processes

Shells and button cap in SLS PA2200 — it is support-free, tough enough to carry the snap lip, and its natural finish is the off-white matte the design asked for with no dyeing step. Grilles in SLA resin, which is the only way to get the perforation. Every bureau below runs both, so this is one supplier and two line items, not two suppliers.

If splitting is inconvenient, order everything in SLA and accept a more brittle, UV-sensitive shell. What you cannot do is order everything in SLS or MJF — the grilles would come back solid.

What to specify on the order

  • Units are millimetres, scale 1 : 1. The STLs are exported that way, but bureau uploaders sometimes guess inches.
  • No hollowing and no infill reduction on the shells — the 2.0 mm walls are the design, and an automatic hollowing pass would breach them.
  • Leave the mic waveguide bores and the button bore unfinished. No vapour smoothing: it rounds over the port mouth and shifts the resonance calculated in §03.
  • Natural finish, undyed, on the PA2200 shells. Dyeing black is the usual house default and would lose the off-white the design calls for.
  • Ask for the grilles to be printed flat with the holes vertical, so the perforation resolves in XY where SLA resolution is highest.

11 Open issues

Findings from measuring the model against the original case-design brief. E items are enclosure, F firmware, D design intent. Numbering is specific to this revision.

E1

Grille clearance is too tight for any process

The grille disc is ⌀8.4 mm in a ⌀8.5 mm recess — 0.05 mm per side. The disc prints SLA at ±0.25 mm and the recess prints SLS at ±0.3 mm, so worst-case stack-up is an interference fit of over half a millimetre. Open the recess to ⌀9.0 mm and bond the disc in with cyanoacrylate; a glued joint tolerates the slop, a press fit does not. Alternatively keep ⌀8.5 and source a laser-cut metal mesh disc, which is what this part wants to be anyway.

E2

Button stem clearance is below the process minimum

The ⌀8.0 mm stem runs in a ⌀8.4 mm bore — 0.20 mm per side. Standard guidance for a sliding fit in SLS is 0.3–0.5 mm per side, and with both parts at ±0.3 mm the button can bind closed or rattle loose depending on which way the tolerances land. Open the bore to ⌀8.8 mm, giving 0.4 mm nominal per side. The ⌀9.6 mm retaining flange still covers it, so nothing else in the assembly changes.

This is a new finding created by the button itself — worth fixing in the model before the first order goes out, since it costs nothing to change now and a reprint later.

D1

Side-face controls break the symmetry the ends preserve

USB-C, the power slide switch and the LED all sit on the +Y face. The two ends remain perfect mirrors and the mute button is on the centre line, so the core idea survives — but the pod now has a front and a back even though it has no left and right. Worth a conscious decision rather than an accident of layout.

F1

VAD threshold will over-trigger at the obvious setting

A threshold 6 dB above a 40 dBA room floor puts the trigger at 46 dB SPL, which fires on normal speech out to 5.0 m — far past the 1.8 m the mic can usefully transcribe. The device would burn power and card space recording the corridor. Set the threshold to 55 dB SPL, which matches the trigger radius to the usable radius at 1.78 m.

Trigger range against threshold: 46 dB → 5.01 m · 50 dB → 3.16 m · 55 dB → 1.78 m · 58 dB → 1.26 m.

F2

Sample rate is 16 129 Hz, not 16 000 Hz

See §05. Harmless to audio quality, 29 s/h of drift against wall-clock. Label files with the real rate.

F3

microSD write pattern dominates recording power

See §06. Buffering to 32 kB blocks and power-gating the card between writes is worth roughly 0.4 mA of the 5.02 mA recording draw — the Opus switch in §08 already took the larger share.

F4

Mute must gate capture, not just discard samples

The button is a logic input, not a hardware disconnect — the microphones stay powered when muted. For the mute to be worth anything, firmware has to stop the I²S transfer and drop the buffer at the point of capture, not filter it downstream. It should also be unambiguous in the file: either no segment is written while muted, or the manifest records the muted interval explicitly. Silently writing near-silence is the one behaviour to avoid, because it makes a muted recording indistinguishable from a quiet one.

Deployment, not hardware

This device sits between a patient and a clinician and stores their conversation. The hardware supports doing that properly — a visible indicator and a deliberate physical control — but the hardware is only half of it. Consent laws vary by jurisdiction and generally turn on whether every party knows recording is happening. The workflow that makes this appropriate to use is someone saying "I'm going to record this" out loud before the switch moves, with the LED as the standing reminder and the centre button as the control anyone at the table can reach. A mute that only the operator can find is not a consent affordance — putting it on the centre line, in plain view of both parties, is what makes pressing it mean something.
